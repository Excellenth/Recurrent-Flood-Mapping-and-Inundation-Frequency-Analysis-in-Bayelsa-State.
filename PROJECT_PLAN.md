# Sentinel-1 SAR + ML Flood Mapping and Prediction — Bayelsa State

**Target output:** single comprehensive journal paper (e.g. *Remote Sensing*, *Int. J. Applied Earth Observation and Geoinformation*, *Natural Hazards*, *GIScience & Remote Sensing*).

## Scope note on study period
Sentinel-1 launched April 2014; usable IW-mode coverage over the Niger Delta is reliable from **2015 onward**. The 2012 Nigeria flood cannot be captured by SAR and is used only as **motivating/background context** (cited via NEMA/SEMA records and literature), not as a training or validation target. All SAR-based analysis (RF training, inundation frequency, LSTM) anchors on **2015–present**, with **2019, 2020, and 2022** as the primary flood-year targets (major recorded events, all within Sentinel-1 coverage, all with NEMA/SEMA records available for cross-validation). This will be stated explicitly as a limitation/scope boundary in the paper.

## Phase 2 — RF Classification (Objective 2)
**Key finding from Phase 1 validation (2022 event, JRC-masked comparison):** backscatter in "never water" upland/forest pixels (~94% of sampled AOI) *increased* ~0.36 dB (both VV/VH) during the flood window vs. dry-season baseline — the double-bounce signature of flooded mangrove/swamp forest, not the classic open-water specular decrease. Seasonal/permanent water pixels showed near-zero change (already wet year-round). Implication: a simple low-backscatter threshold will miss most real flood extent here. Training samples must explicitly cover both regimes — open-water decrease *and* flooded-vegetation increase — as distinct signatures rather than assuming one uniform "flood = dark" rule.
- Feature stack: VV, VH, VH/VV ratio, GLCM texture, DEM/slope, distance-to-river
- Training samples: flood/non-flood polygons from known flood dates (thresholding + visual interpretation, e.g. Otsu on VV histogram as a starting point) — must include flooded-forest (double-bounce) examples, not just open-water

**Status (as of first training run):** semi-automated pseudo-labels generated from the event-vs-baseline VH anomaly (asymmetric 5th/90th percentile thresholds, per the double-bounce finding above), constrained by elevation (≤10m) and river distance (≤2km flood-plausible / ≥3km high-confidence non-flood) to keep only high-confidence pixels — ambiguous mid-range anomaly pixels are dropped rather than force-labeled. 1800 samples pooled across 2019/2020/2022 (balanced 900/900).

**Data leakage caught and fixed:** initial run included `elevation`/`river_dist` as classifier *features* as well as label-generation constraints — model hit 100% accuracy with 76% of feature importance on `river_dist` alone, i.e. it was just re-deriving the labeling threshold rather than learning a SAR signature. Fixed by excluding those two bands from the classifier's `FEATURE_BANDS` (kept only as label-generation constraints). Corrected held-out test accuracy: **74.6%** (scikit-learn RF, 70/30 split, balanced classes), with feature importance well-distributed across VH/VV/texture/slope — no shortcut feature dominating. This is a real number, but still only a random split of the same pseudo-labeled pool, not independent validation — that's Phase 4's job.

**2022 full-composite classification sanity check:** ~49.9% of Bayelsa's area (4,890 of 9,807 km²) classified as flooded during the 2022 event window. Order-of-magnitude plausible given ~1.3M people displaced in the worst-hit state of Nigeria's worst flood in a decade, but likely has an upward bias: the pseudo-labeling approach can't fully separate "genuinely newly flooded" pixels from "permanently swampy/mangrove forest that already looks flood-like in SAR year-round." Treat this as a preliminary estimate pending Phase 4 independent validation, not a final number.

**Wet-season inundation frequency map (2015-2025, Aug-Nov, 44 months) — completed and exported** to Drive (`bayelsa_wetseason_flood_frequency_2015_2025`, scale=60m) plus summary stats read back via a GEE table asset (Drive access wasn't available this session; asset export sidesteps that). Results: mean frequency 46.2%, median 36.4%, **p95/p99/max saturated at 100%**.

**Update (file downloaded locally to `data/frequency_map/`, 2026-07-10):** Drive had two files with the same name -- `bayelsa_wetseason_flood_frequency_2015_2025.tif` (4 bands, the corrected final version) and a `(1)`-suffixed duplicate (2 bands, the original *unmasked* export from before the saturation bug was found and fixed). Verified by band count before use; the 2-band buggy duplicate was deleted. Also found and fixed: GEE exported masked pixels as literal NaN without tagging NaN as the file's nodata value, so `rasterio`'s masking silently failed until this was set explicitly -- fixed in place. Authoritative full-resolution statistics (1,690,237 valid pixels, not an approximate `bestEffort` sample): mean 51.5%, median 50.0%, max 88.6% (matches the earlier GEE p99 almost exactly, a good cross-check). Slightly higher than the earlier coarse GEE estimate, as expected from comparing an approximate sample to the real complete census -- this file is now the authoritative number.

**Real limitation surfaced by this result, not just noise:** a genuinely episodic flood signal shouldn't hit 100% in every single sampled wet-season month for a decade — that pattern is the signature of permanent water/swamp forest (rivers, tidal creeks, year-round mangrove) being classified as "flood" regardless of whether an actual flood event is occurring, which the current classifier can't distinguish from real transient inundation. Corroborating evidence: the 2022 extreme-event estimate (49.9% of state area) is barely above the 11-year wet-season average (46.2%) — if the classifier separated event severity, 2022 should stand out clearly above typical wet-season months, not sit close to the mean. **Recommended refinement before treating this as a final deliverable:** mask out JRC permanent water/high-occurrence pixels from the frequency map to isolate genuinely episodic flood-risk zones from the year-round wetland baseline, and/or recalibrate the anomaly threshold against a multi-year normal rather than each year's own Jan-Mar baseline.

**GEE compute-cost lessons (kept here so they aren't relearned):** GEE's interactive/synchronous compute has a ~5min timeout. GLCM texture and the Refined Lee filter are the expensive operations — evaluating either region-wide (as `stratifiedSample`/`sampleRegions` must, since they scan the whole area to find candidate pixels) at fine scale (20m) repeatedly hit this ceiling. Fixes that worked: (1) pull only the narrow event/baseline date windows, not a full year; (2) pick sample point *locations* from a cheap band stack (no GLCM) first, then extract the expensive full feature stack only at those point locations via `sampleRegions`; (3) sample at a coarser scale (60m, justified since despeckled SAR is already smoother than that from the 7x7 filter window) rather than 20m — this was the highest-leverage fix. Async batch exports (`ee.batch.Export.*`) don't have this timeout and are the right tool for genuinely large computations (used for the 6 SAR composites in Phase 1). Further lessons from the frequency-map stats export: `Export.table.*` always requires non-null feature geometry, even for a single summary-stats row with no real spatial meaning (give it a throwaway point, e.g. `aoi.centroid()`) — this bit both the training-sample export and the stats export. Reducer outputs must be scalar to export as a table property; `fixedHistogram`'s 2D array output fails encoding. And calling `reduceRegion` multiple times on the same large lazily-defined image (e.g. 4 separate calls on the same 44-month classification result) seems to force full independent re-evaluation each time rather than reusing a shared result — this caused an out-of-memory failure that a single combined-reducer call avoided.
- Train RF (`ee.Classifier.smileRandomForest` in GEE, or scikit-learn locally on exported features)
- Classify each date → aggregate into inundation frequency map (% of dates flooded per pixel)
- Identify chronically flood-affected zones (low-lying communities, river corridors)

## Phase 3 — LSTM Prediction (Objective 3) ✅ prototype done
**Scoping trade-off (deliberate, not a hidden shortcut):** given the GEE noncommercial compute quota was exhausted during Phase 2 (restricted until ~Aug 1), full per-pixel classification across the historical record (as used for the Phase 2 frequency map) wasn't repeated for every month back to 2015 -- too expensive under restricted throughput. Instead: 8 LGA-level zones (FAO GAUL level-2), monthly zonal mean VV/VH (raw, undespeckled -- zonal averaging over many pixels already suppresses speckle, so the expensive Refined Lee/GLCM pipeline wasn't needed) + CHIRPS rainfall, 2015-2025 (132 months/zone, 1056 rows, `data/lstm_zonal_timeseries.csv`). This predicts a **continuous zonal backscatter/flood-risk-trend signal**, not a classified per-pixel flood extent map. Worth stating explicitly in the paper as a scoping decision.

- Data gap: Jan-Mar 2015 has no Sentinel-1 scenes for this orbit (pre-dates stable revisit pattern) -- dropped those 24 rows rather than impute a genuine satellite gap.
- Sequences: 6-month lookback window of [VV, VH, rainfall] -> predict next month's VH (chosen as target since Phase 1 showed both the open-water decrease *and* flooded-vegetation increase manifest most clearly in VH).
- **Chronological train/test split** (train 2015-2023, test 2024-2025) -- not random, to avoid leaking future information.
- Simple 1-layer LSTM (32 units) + dense head, trained locally (TensorFlow/Keras, no GEE). **Test result: MAE 0.116 dB vs. 0.148 dB for a naive "same as last month" persistence baseline (beats it by ~22%), R²=0.895.** Model saved to `outputs/lstm_zonal_vh_forecast.keras`.
- **Flood-risk interpretation layer added** (`notebooks/lstm_flood_risk_interpretation.py`): first attempt used a two-point benchmark (mean VH during known flood months vs. dry-season baseline, ~6-9 points/zone) — the gap (0.19-0.35 dB) turned out smaller than the LSTM's own prediction error (0.116 dB MAE), so the resulting index was mostly noise (June scored above Sep-Nov). Fixed by using each zone's percentile rank within its full training-period VH history (105 months/zone) instead — far more stable. Now correctly separates dry season (Jan-Apr, Dec: index 0.04-0.21) from wet season (May-Nov: 0.37-0.52).
- **Resolved (2026-08-14), without NIHSA data:** within the wet season, June/July ranked above the NEMA-reported peak months (Sep-Nov) in the LSTM's *forecast*-based risk index. `notebooks/lstm_risk_index_actual_vs_forecast.py` computes the same percentile-rank index from the *actual observed* VH at the same test-period zone-months (same training-period reference distribution) instead of the forecast, isolating whether this is a real physical signal or a forecasting artifact. Result: the actual/ground-truth risk index peaks in Sep/Oct (0.523/0.530) — matching NEMA exactly — while Jun/Jul are lower in the ground truth (0.428/0.493) than the LSTM forecast (0.514/0.520). The LSTM systematically under-predicts the sharp Sep-Oct peak and over-smooths toward Jun/Jul (also over-predicts Nov: forecast 0.462 vs actual 0.214). Confirmed as a genuine model limitation (regression-to-the-mean smoothing of a sharp seasonal peak, typical of a short-lookback LSTM under MSE training), not an early-onset physical signal. NIHSA discharge data would still help *correct* the under-prediction but was not needed to diagnose it.
- **5-year-interval maps propagated into paper (2026-08-16):** 2016/2020/2025 comparison (2016 substituting for unreliable 2015, see above) added as Figure 2c in `PAPER_DRAFT.md` Section 4.2, distinguished clearly from the 2019/2020/2022 documented-events comparison -- mild downward trend noted (58.6% -> 54.5% -> 51.2%) but explicitly flagged as suggestive only (3 points, one substituted). `bayelsa_2016_flood_extent.tif` and `bayelsa_2025_flood_extent.tif` added to `outputs/Bayelsa_Flood_SAR.gpkg` (verified against source). Kappa statistics (RF held-out 0.493, Sentinel-2 validation 0.221) and the LSTM LGA choropleth (Figure 6) also propagated same session. Docx and gpkg both regenerated.
- **LSTM choropleth added (2026-08-14):** the LSTM forecasts a zonal time series by design -- no per-pixel raster output exists or should exist. `notebooks/lstm_lga_choropleth.py` joins the zonal risk index (real 2024-2025 test-period mean and the 2026-2030 baseline mean) to the 8 LGA polygons, producing `data/aoi/bayelsa_lgas_lstm_risk.geojson` and a 2-panel choropleth figure. Added to `PAPER_DRAFT.md` as Figure 6, and as a new `lgas_lstm_risk_index` layer in `outputs/Bayelsa_Flood_SAR.gpkg`.
- **2026-2030 SDG baseline added (2026-08-14):** `notebooks/lstm_extrapolate_2030.py` extends the LSTM to a stable 2026-2030 "no-trend" baseline framed against SDG Targets 1.5, 11.5, and 13.1 (all explicit 2030-horizon targets). First attempt recursively fed forecasts back in as future input and drifted (July predicted VH rose monotonically 2026->2030 with identical inputs each year -- a compounding-error artifact, discarded). Fixed version builds every future month's lookback purely from historical per-calendar-month climatology, never from a prior prediction -- output is identical every year by construction (verified: July VH = -14.116 dB in all 5 years), a flat reference line, not a genuine forecast. Explicitly excludes climate change, sea-level rise, land-use change. Propagated into `PAPER_DRAFT.md` as new content in Sections 3.6, 4.4 (new Figure 5), and a new Section 5.5 connecting the study's findings to the three SDG targets (verified exact UN wording before citing). Docx regenerated.
- **Reproduction verified (2026-08-14):** `train_lstm.py` has no fixed random seed, so it was re-run as an independent check rather than assumed correct. Fresh run: MAE 0.111 dB, R²=0.899 (vs. published 0.116 dB/0.895) — same conclusion, small expected variation from random weight initialization, not a discrepancy. The June/July-over-Sep/Oct pattern reproduced closely with the freshly retrained model too (Jul 0.534, Jun 0.483 vs. Sep 0.419, Oct 0.418), confirming it's a structural model behavior, not a fluke of one training run. The original model/prediction files (matching the paper's exact published numbers) were restored as canonical afterward — the fresh run was for verification only, not to replace the reported results.
- **Per-pixel 2026-2030 forecast added (2026-09-30, under same-day submission deadline):** the LGA-level LSTM baseline was downscaled to pixel resolution (`notebooks/lstm_pixel_forecast_2026_2030.py`) rather than training a genuine per-pixel LSTM, which would repeat the exact compute cost (~1.69M pixels, multi-year monthly lookback each) that forced zonal granularity in the first place, with no time to recover from a fresh quota exhaustion. Method: statistical downscaling (delta method) — `pixel_forecast = flood_frequency_pixel + (risk_index_baseline_2026_2030[LGA] - risk_index_test_2024_2025[LGA])`, clipped to [0,1]. The zonal LSTM risk index supplies the temporal forecast signal; the existing wet-season flood-frequency raster supplies the spatial pattern. **First attempt used a multiplicative ratio instead of additive delta and was rejected**: it pushed 35.7% of all pixels past the 1.0 ceiling (flood_frequency is a bounded [0,1] proportion with mean already 0.515, and multiplicative scale factors of 1.3-2.0x per LGA blew straight through it) — exactly the already flood-prone areas losing all spatial differentiation. Switched to additive delta (the correct choice for a bounded proportion; multiplicative ratios are for unbounded positive quantities like rainfall), which cut ceiling saturation to 12.1%. Verified the remaining ceiling pixels are a genuine result, not a residual artifact: they average 0.85 base frequency already (top of the natural distribution) and concentrate in the LGAs with the largest projected increases (Nembe 14.2% of zone at ceiling, Ekeremor 9.8%, Brass 8.6%) rather than being spread uniformly or tied to the permanent-water mask. Output: `outputs/geotiffs/bayelsa_pixel_forecast_2026_2030.tif` (mean 0.705, median 0.698), added to the GeoPackage as `pixel_forecast_2026_2030` and previewed in `outputs/figures/pixel_forecast_2026_2030_preview.png`. **Must be stated in the paper as a spatial disaggregation of the already-documented zonal forecast, not an independent pixel-level prediction** — it inherits the zonal baseline's own "flat, no further climate/land-use trend" assumption (Phase 3) and assumes each LGA's internal spatial flood pattern stays fixed while only its overall level shifts.

## Phase 4 — Validation (Objective 4)

**Status: initial pass done, 2022-11-03.** Compared the SAR flood classification (2022 event composite) against Sentinel-2 NDWI (cloud-masked 8-tile mosaic, 14.5% cloud on best tile, McFeeters threshold) as an independent reference. Stratified sample, 1000 points/class:
- Where S2 sees open water: SAR agrees 98% of the time (980/1000) — high recall, rarely misses genuine water.
- Where S2 sees dry land: SAR still calls "flood" 76% of the time (759/1000) — the concerning number, but expected to be partly (not wholly) explained by the Phase 1 double-bounce finding, since NDWI can't see flooded vegetation under canopy.
- Visual spot-check of 5 disagreement locations: all 5 show dense forest/vegetation canopy, none show bare/dry land — supports the flooded-vegetation explanation over a genuine over-classification error, though one location showed residual cloud contamination the SCL mask missed, meaning the 76% figure is an approximate upper bound (some of that disagreement is data-quality noise, not real land-cover mismatch).

**Caveat carried through:** NDWI/S2 only detects open water, same canopy blind spot as JRC (see Phase 1/2 notes). This validation confirms the open-water portion of the classifier is reliable; it cannot by itself confirm or refute accuracy on flooded vegetation, which is most of what makes this AOI hard. A stricter cloud mask (dilated SCL buffer) would sharpen this number if pursued further.

**NEMA narrative cross-check (LGA-level, 2022) — RESOLVED (2026-08-14), previously inconclusive.** Media/NEMA reports named Yenagoa LGA as worst-hit in 2022. The original JRC-only-masked zonal check put Yenagoa only 5th of 8 (43-45%), with coastal/riverine Nembe (~73%) and Brass (~69%) on top — flagged at the time as likely still dominated by swamp-forest double-bounce conflation that JRC masking alone can't remove, with re-running against the full SAR-persistent-wetland mask left as an open item. That mask is now available locally (frequency map downloaded) and was applied: `notebooks/lga_zonal_check_masked.py` shows Nembe's and Brass's apparent dominance was indeed mostly chronic-wetland conflation (54.3%/50.9% of their area excluded), while Yenagoa (only 16.7% excluded) barely changed — under the corrected dual mask, Yenagoa's flood % (33.7%) is 3rd of 8 and close to the top two, directly consistent with the NEMA narrative. See Phase 2 update below and `PAPER_DRAFT.md` Section 3.7 for full detail. This also flipped the Phase 5 priority ranking (see below).

**Update — pixel-level population weighting completed (2026-08-14).** The WorldPop raster export finished once the GEE quota reset; `notebooks/phase5_corridor_population_weighting.py` buffers each significant corridor segment (≥5px) by 1km and sums WorldPop population within that catchment directly from the native-resolution raster (vector masking, no resampling). Full ranking: `outputs/tables/phase5_corridor_population_weighting.csv`. This also revises the earlier Phase 5 framing that Yenegoa's low corridor coverage means embankments are "the wrong intervention" there — its few corridor segments are in fact unusually high-value on a per-segment basis and may still warrant targeted embankment investment alongside broader drainage improvements.
